Why Getting a Will Has Always Been Too Hard — And Why 59% of Brits Are Still Paying the Price

The barriers keeping millions of UK families unprotected have nothing to do with laziness. They're structural, expensive, and deliberate. Here's the full story — and why it's finally changing.

📄

Here's a number that should stop you in your tracks: 59% of UK adults do not have a valid will. That's more than 31 million people — parents, homeowners, business owners, people with savings, pensions, and people who love them — leaving their estates entirely to chance.

It's tempting to chalk this up to apathy. But the data tells a more complicated story. The real reason so few people make a will is that the traditional system has made it genuinely, unnecessarily hard. Confusing, expensive, slow, and — for most people — deeply off-putting.

This article unpacks the barriers that have kept millions of families unprotected for generations, explains exactly what happens if you die without a will in the UK, and shows why the arrival of proper online will writing services is the most significant shift in estate planning in decades.

"Two-thirds of UK adults either don't have a will or have one that's out of date."

— Will Aid, 2025

The Traditional Hurdles: Why Writing a Will Has Always Been a Nightmare

1. The Cost Shock

Ask most people why they haven't written their will and cost comes up almost immediately. Surveys consistently show that more than half of non-will holders cite cost as a factor. This isn't entirely irrational — traditionally, using a will writing solicitor for even a straightforward single will could set you back anywhere from £150 to over £400. Mirror wills for couples pushed that figure even higher.

And those are the published fixed-fee prices. Any complexity — business interests, overseas assets, discretionary trusts, blended families — and you're suddenly billed by the hour at rates between £200 and £400+ per hour in major cities. For many people, the prospect of an open-ended legal bill for something they don't fully understand is enough to put the whole thing off indefinitely.

2. The Appointment Treadmill

Traditional will writing services required you to physically visit a solicitor's office. Usually more than once. First to discuss your wishes, then to review the draft, then to sign in front of witnesses. Each appointment meant taking time off work, arranging childcare, travelling — and then waiting weeks between visits while the document worked its way through the firm's caseload.

For working parents, carers, or anyone with a busy life, that's not a minor inconvenience. It's a genuine barrier. And so the will goes back on the to-do list, where it sits for another year.

3. The Jargon Wall

Legal language exists to be precise. But precision has a dark side: it excludes. Concepts like probate, residuary estate, testamentary capacity, intestacy, and per stirpes distributions are second nature to solicitors and completely opaque to almost everyone else. When the document that's supposed to protect your family reads like a foreign language, trust evaporates. People either don't engage, or they sign something they don't fully understand.

Research shows that legal confusion remains a major barrier even among people who have started the process — let alone those who haven't.

4. The Mortality Taboo

There's a reason people say they haven't "got around" to writing their will. Writing a will forces you to confront the fact that you're going to die. For many people — particularly younger adults — that psychological barrier is very real. Combined with the practical friction described above, it gives procrastination the perfect excuse to win.

54% of people without a will admit that inertia — simply not getting round to it — is the main reason they haven't acted. When a process is difficult, inconvenient, and emotionally uncomfortable, the path of least resistance always wins.

5. The "I Don't Have Enough to Bother" Myth

One of the most persistent misconceptions in wills and probate is that wills are only for the wealthy. That they're for people with stately homes and investment portfolios, not ordinary families with a mortgage and a pension.

This couldn't be more wrong. If you have any assets — a home, savings, a car, personal possessions, a digital footprint — you have an estate. If you have children, you need to name a guardian. If you have a partner you're not married to, you especially need a will. The law will not protect you or them automatically.

"Cohabitants — so-called 'common law spouses' — have no automatic right to inherit under UK intestacy rules, regardless of how long the relationship has lasted."

— Citizens Advice / Rules of Intestacy

What Actually Happens If You Die Without a Will in the UK

Dying without a will in England and Wales means your estate is distributed according to the rules of intestacy — a rigid legal framework that has no interest in what you actually wanted.

Here's what that looks like in practice:

If you're married or in a civil partnership and your estate is worth more than £322,000, your spouse receives the first £322,000 plus half of what remains. The other half goes to your children — not as a lump sum they can use, but as a share of the estate subject to the full weight of the probate process.

If you're unmarried, your partner receives nothing. Zero. Regardless of how long you've been together, whether you own a home together, whether you have children. Your estate passes to your children — or if you have none, to your parents, then your siblings. Your partner may have to fight in court for any share of assets they helped build.

If you have no spouse, children, or living relatives, your entire estate passes to the Crown. The government inherits everything.

None of this is hypothetical. These situations play out in probate courts across the UK every year — grieving families torn apart by a document that was never written. Estate planning isn't morbid. It's one of the most caring things you can do for the people you love.

The 59% Problem: Why This Is a National Issue

The UK's will writing gap is not a personal failing. It's a systemic one. For decades, the legal profession has had no competitive pressure to make wills more accessible. Fixed-fee pricing was opaque. Online services were either non-existent or legally dubious. And the professional bodies that could have pushed for change had little incentive to disrupt a profitable status quo.

The result? Almost 6 in 10 adults are legally unprotected. Their families are unprotected. And 48% of people surveyed say they worry that the absence of a will could trigger arguments or disputes among those they leave behind — disputes that a properly drafted, legally binding will would prevent entirely.

Do I Need a Will? The Short Answer

If any of the following apply to you, the answer is yes:

You own property, even jointly. You have children. You have a partner you're not married to. You have savings, a pension, or life insurance. You have strong views about who should receive your possessions. You want to leave anything to charity. You want to specify your funeral wishes. You have digital assets — crypto, accounts, subscriptions — that have real-world value.

In other words: almost everyone reading this needs a will. And the only reason not to have one is the friction that has historically made getting one so difficult. That friction no longer needs to exist.

The Bottom Line

The UK's will-writing crisis is not a crisis of apathy. It's a crisis of access. Decades of expensive appointments, impenetrable legal language, and a system designed around solicitors' convenience rather than clients' needs have left millions of families dangerously exposed.

But the solution is simple, affordable, and available right now. A legally binding will — drafted properly, stored securely, and written in plain English — is no longer the preserve of the wealthy or the organised.
